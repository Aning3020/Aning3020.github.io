The Missing or Unfinished Piece in Sabalenka’s Grand Slam Finals

**Hi Aryna’s team,

I’m writing as a long-time WTA tennis fan and someone who has followed Aryna’s matches closely for years. I wanted to share a personal observation with a great deal of respect, care, and admiration for everything she has already achieved.**

1. What seems to happen in major finals

While watching the recent Australian Open final—especially when Aryna was up 3–0 in the third set—my instinctive reaction was not simply confidence, but concern.

I had felt something similar before, and it seems connected to a recurring pattern that appears precisely because she cares so deeply and wants so much.

Against equally elite opponents in major finals, when Aryna gains a lead, there can sometimes be a subtle shift: the focus seems to move from playing freely to protecting what has already been gained.

Tension rises. Decision-making can tighten. And the fear of losing may begin to compete with the instinct to simply keep playing.

At times, it can look as though an internal message becomes very strong:

“I must not lose this.”

And when the possibility of losing carries not only competitive disappointment, but also shame, self-judgment, or a threat to identity, the pressure can become enormous.

I can imagine how heavy that must feel in a moment when the entire world is watching.

2. The missing piece may be internal safety

What may sound paradoxical, but feels to me like an unfinished or missing piece in Aryna’s game in major finals, is this:

Aryna may actually play her best tennis when she allows herself the possibility of losing, rather than feeling that she must win or must remain fully in control.

More precisely, the missing piece may be internal safety.

By that, I mean the ability to feel safe enough to miss a point, face an opponent playing exceptionally well, or even lose a match—without any of those moments becoming a threat to her sense of self.

If that safety is there, freedom, trust, instinct, and full expression can remain available even under the highest stakes.

In finals, she may actually need more internal space and more safety, not less—a more expansive internal state rather than a narrowed one.

Not in a way that weakens her ambition or competitive fire, but in a way that frees them.

3. What internal safety could make possible

But what if losing were no longer met with shame, self-attack, or harsh judgment?

What if it could instead be held with self-acceptance, self-respect, and a deeper sense of wholeness?

When an athlete feels internally safe—safe to miss, safe to adjust, safe to be imperfect, safe to remain present even when momentum shifts—the system can begin to soften.

And from that safety, more freedom becomes available:

  • the freedom to commit fully to a shot,
  • the freedom to trust instinct,
  • the freedom to respond rather than control,
  • and the freedom to let power move through the body without interruption.

With that reversal in mindset, I believe an enormous amount of internal space could open up.

And from that space—from greater internal safety and a less judgmental inner environment—I believe even more of Aryna’s extraordinary ability could become available to her when the stakes are highest.

4. Integration, not less ambition

I deeply recognize that competitiveness, hunger, discipline, intensity, and extremely high standards are part of what has carried Aryna to this level.

Those qualities are powerful. They are part of her greatness.

I’m not suggesting that she abandon any of them.

But perhaps, at this stage of her career, the next step is not to become even harder on herself.it is to integrate that extraordinary drive with greater self-trust, self-compassion, self-forgiveness, and more room for trial and error.

Not because she should want the victory less.
But because she may be able to pursue it more freely.

The next step may not be more control, but more internal safety and more internal room—so that freedom remains available even when winning is already within reach.

And making space for that truth may paradoxically make it easier for her to play the kind of tennis that wins finals.

I truly believe that through this kind of integration, championships may arrive more naturally—not through pressure, self-criticism, and impossibly high standards alone,

but from a state in which all of Aryna’s strength, instinct, courage, and freedom remain available to her.

She has already built so much.

With best wishes, care, and admiration ⭐
A devoted tennis fan and Aryna supporter, always believing 🐅

This email was written on February 2nd, right after the 2026 Australian Open. I didn’t send it at the time, but after hearing about the US Open recently, I felt I should share it.

I honestly don’t know whether you’ll ever see it. But I do believe the perspective I’m sharing here has real value.

It comes from years of self-study in psychology, neuroscience, the nervous system, and some Eastern philosophy—much of it shaped by my own experience of adversity.

(## The cost of harsh self-criticism

I also notice that after missed balls, Aryna can sometimes respond to herself with a very intense, almost aggressive level of criticism.

I understand that this may partly come from the same competitiveness and high standards that have helped carry her so far.

But in a final, that same force can also become costly.

It drains energy, turns the inner dialogue against her, and can allow one missed point to grow into doubt, frustration, or even catastrophic thinking about the whole match.

It also leaves almost no room for error.

And tennis is a sport built on error.

Matches can last one, two, or three hours. Very few are truly decided by one missed ball alone.

A missed point is usually just one point.

If every mistake begins to feel like evidence that something is going wrong, there is less inner room to reset, recover, and fully return to the next one.
